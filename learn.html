Short, plain-English definitions of the words you'll see every day on a trading platform.

Pip

The smallest standard price move. For most pairs 0.0001 (4th decimal); for JPY pairs 0.01 (2nd decimal).

Pipette

One tenth of a pip — the 5th decimal on most pairs (0.00001).

Lot

A standard unit of trade size. One standard lot = 100,000 units of the base currency.

Mini lot

0.10 lots = 10,000 units of the base currency.

Micro lot

0.01 lots = 1,000 units of the base currency.

Base currency

The first currency in a pair. In EUR/USD, EUR is the base.

Quote currency

The second currency in a pair. It tells you how much one unit of the base costs.

Bid

The price at which you can sell right now.

Ask

The price at which you can buy right now.

Spread

The gap between bid and ask — the broker's main trading cost, measured in pips.

Leverage

A ratio (e.g. 1:100) letting you open a position much larger than your deposit. It changes the locked deposit, not the risk.

Margin

The deposit the broker locks to keep your position open. Not a fee — your own money held as security.

Free margin

Account money minus locked margin — what's available for new trades.

Margin call

A broker warning that losses are eating into your margin. Add money or close positions.

Stop-out

The broker automatically closes losing positions when your account can't cover the losses.

Balance

All your account money, including closed-trade profits and losses.

Equity

Balance plus/minus open-position profit or loss — the real value of your account right now.

Stop-loss

An order that closes your trade automatically at a chosen price to limit loss.

Take-profit

An order that closes your trade automatically at a chosen price to lock in a gain.

Swap / rollover

A small fee or credit for holding a position past the trading day. Swap-free (Islamic) accounts exist.

Slippage

Your order filled at a different price than you clicked — the market moved fast.

Long / Short

Long = buying, expecting the base currency to rise. Short = selling, expecting it to fall.

HYPOTHETICAL EXAMPLES — every number below is made up for teaching only. Real prices move differently, and real trading also has spreads and swap fees.

Leverage and margin on a $1,000 account (hypothetical)

Setup: balance $1,000, leverage 1:100 (1% margin required). You open 1 mini lot = 10,000 units of EUR/USD at 1.1000.

Margin required: position value = 10,000 × 1.1000 = $11,000. Margin locked = 1% of $11,000 = $110. So $110 is locked and $890 is free margin — the position is 11× bigger than the account.

A 1% move against you: 1% of $11,000 = $110 lost — 11% of the account gone from a 1% price move.

Risk warning: leverage magnifies losses, not just profits. A small move in the wrong direction can wipe out a large part of your account.

Pip value basics (hypothetical, USD-quote pairs)

  • 1 standard lot (100,000 units): 1 pip = $10
  • 1 mini lot (10,000 units): 1 pip = $1
  • 1 micro lot (1,000 units): 1 pip = $0.10

Stop-loss position sizing — risking 1% (hypothetical)

Setup: account $1,000, risk 1% = $10 maximum loss on one trade.

  1. Choose the stop distance first: your plan says 20 pips.
  2. On a EUR/USD mini lot, 20 pips ≈ $20 — too much for a $10 risk.
  3. Shrink the size: 0.05 lots = $0.50 per pip. 20 pips × $0.50 = $10 risk — matches your limit.

Rule to remember: decide the risk first (dollars you can afford to lose), then the stop distance, then the size. Never the other way round.

Trading plan template — fill in before trading real money

  1. Goal — why am I trading, and what am I learning first? (e.g. follow a plan for 30 trades)
  2. Markets — which pairs or instruments, and nothing else
  3. Session — which hours of the day, in your country time
  4. Setup — exactly what must appear on the chart before entry, as a checklist
  5. Entry rules — what confirms the entry, and what cancels it
  6. Stop-loss rule — maximum stop distance and where it sits relative to the setup
  7. Take-profit rule — where you exit with a gain; never "let it run and hope"
  8. Risk per trade — fixed percentage of the account (e.g. 1%), the same every trade
  9. Daily/weekly limits — max trades per day, and the loss level where you stop for the day
  10. Review habit — when you read your journal weekly, and the one question you ask of it

Journaling guide — record per trade, and why

  1. Date and time — spot which sessions work best or worst for you
  2. Pair and direction — shows if you favour certain pairs
  3. Entry and exit price — the raw facts
  4. Lot size and pip value — check you risked what you planned
  5. Stop-loss and take-profit — were they set before entry, or moved in panic?
  6. Result in pips and in money — money alone hides bad habits; pips show decision quality
  7. Screenshot or chart note — a picture catches mistakes words miss
  8. Emotion and lesson (one line) — how you felt, and one thing to do better next time

Why journal: after 20–30 trades, your journal shows your real patterns — overtrading, moving stops, revenge trades. No memory is honest enough to replace written records.

5 common behavioural mistakes — and one-line fixes

  • Revenge trading (bigger trades right after a loss) — after any loss, stand up for 15 minutes before touching the platform.
  • Moving the stop-loss — decide the stop before entry; if you move it, the trade is cancelled in your journal.
  • Overtrading — set a max number of trades per day and log every breach.
  • Risking too much — risk is a fixed percentage of the account, the same on every trade, no exceptions.
  • Trading without a plan — no checklist, no trade. If it's not in the plan, it doesn't exist.

Broker due-diligence checklist — 10 checks before you deposit

  1. Regulation — which regulator issued the licence? Known names: FCA (UK), ASIC (Australia), CySEC (Cyprus), DFSA (Dubai), SCA (UAE). Check the licence number yourself on the regulator's own website.
  2. Licence matches the company — the name and number on the regulator's site must match the broker, not a look-alike.
  3. Segregated client funds — client money kept in separate bank accounts from the company's own money. Ask for this in writing.
  4. Withdrawal test — deposit a small amount first, then withdraw it. If a small withdrawal is hard, a large one will be harder.
  5. Fee transparency — spreads, commissions, swaps and deposit/withdrawal fees clearly published.
  6. Trading conditions in writing — leverage, margin rules and stop-out level easy to find and understand.
  7. Platform reliability — stable, official platforms (e.g. MT4/MT5). Be cautious of custom apps existing only on the broker's website.
  8. Support quality — ask a hard question before depositing (e.g. "show me your withdrawal policy"). Fast replies for deposits but silence on withdrawals is a bad sign.
  9. Independent reviews — search the broker's name plus "withdrawal problem" on sites the broker doesn't control.
  10. No pressure — a real broker lets you decide at your own speed. Pushy calls to deposit more are a warning, not a service.

Fraud red flags — 10 warning signs

  • Guaranteed returns — nobody can guarantee forex profits. "Guaranteed 10% per month" is always a lie.
  • Pressure to deposit fast — "this offer ends today" is a sales trap, not an opportunity.
  • Unregulated "account managers" — strangers on Telegram/Instagram/WhatsApp offering to trade your money.
  • Bonus lock-ins — big bonuses blocking withdrawals until you trade an impossible volume.
  • Withdrawal problems — easy deposits, then delays, new "verification" steps or surprise fees.
  • Fees to unlock your own money — demands for "tax", "release" or "unlock" fees before withdrawal. Real brokers don't do this.
  • Fake platforms — dashboards showing perfect profits you can't verify anywhere else.
  • Recovery scams — a "recovery expert" offering to get lost money back for an upfront fee.
  • Copycat brokers — websites copying the name, logo or licence number of a real regulated broker.
  • Crypto-only deposits — when the only deposit method can't be reversed.

Withdrawal record-keeping tips

  • Keep every record: deposits, withdrawal requests, confirmations, support chats.
  • One folder per broker, named clearly (broker name + dates).
  • Log request date, approval date, money-received date — delays are the first sign of trouble.
  • Know which KYC documents you submitted, in case of a dispute.
  • Screenshot fee and bonus terms on signup day — pages can change.
  • Export your trading history regularly; it's your evidence if anything goes wrong.
